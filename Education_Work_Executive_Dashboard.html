What this means for the business

—Non-school qualification attainment has climbed every year since 2016, from 10.49m to 12.87m holders nationally — the qualification pipeline is deepening, not just widening.
—Female bachelor-degree-or-above attainment has led male in every year of the 2016–2025 series, and the gap has widened from 4.3 points to 7.3 points — talent supply at the top qualification tier is increasingly female-skewed.
—Apprenticeship starts have stayed flat around 240–270k while unmet demand has climbed from 51k to 66k since 2015 — a pipeline constraint, not a demand problem, worth flagging to leadership.

What this means for the business

—Current enrolment skews female in every state observed — NSW shows 573k female against 503k male — course design and marketing built around a male-leaning cohort works against the actual base.
—Nationally, 73.9% of 15–24 year olds are fully engaged in work or study, but engagement varies meaningfully by state and remoteness — a genuine regional-investment signal.

What this means for the business

—Roughly 81% of the population holds Year 12 or above; a third (33.8%) hold a bachelor's degree or higher — the entry-level cohort without post-school qualifications is shrinking as a share of the population.
—ACT stands well above every other jurisdiction on bachelor-plus attainment (46.4% vs a 33.8% national average) — a genuine regional-investment signal once reliability margins are checked, not noise.

What this means for the business

—Information technology has grown faster than any other field of current study — its share nearly doubled from 3.2% to 5.6% of all current students between 2015 and 2025.
—Management and commerce has lost the most share over the same period, down from 23.1% to 17.3% — a signal that program investment weighted toward legacy business courses may be lagging student demand.

What this means for the business

—Apprenticeship participation has stayed roughly 75–85% male across the full 2015–2025 series — a persistent structural gap rather than a one-year anomaly.
—47% of employed people work in the two highest occupation skill tiers — slightly under half the workforce, with the remainder split across mid- and lower-skill roles.

What this means for the business

—The 2020–2021 period shows a visible dip in apprenticeship proportions and some study series before recovering — worth annotating as a COVID-era disruption rather than treating as a data error.
—Female bachelor-plus holders grew faster than male over the decade — +52% (2.50m to 3.80m) versus +43% (2.08m to 2.98m) for male — a structural, not cyclical, shift in qualification supply.
